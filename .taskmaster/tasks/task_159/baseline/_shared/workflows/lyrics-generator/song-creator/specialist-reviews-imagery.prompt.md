## Step 0 — Identify the Song's MODE

Before applying any show-don't-tell rule, read the lyrics and identify what KIND of song this is. Show-don't-tell is the default advice for lyric imagery, but it is not universal — some modes are built on narration, concession, or inventory, and in those modes telling is the instrument, not the failure.

Common modes and their imagery expectations:

- **Confession** — the narrator is stating a truth they have been avoiding or circling. The power lives in the directness of the admission. A specific admission of what the narrator knows, wants, or did is the mode's spine — flag it as weak *only* if the admission is generic abstract emotion ("I feel so lost"); specific admissions earn their directness.
- **Inventory** — a catalogue of facts about a person, place, or time, often structural setup for later density. Lists of small concrete facts — events, possessions, habits, biographical details — are doing legitimate structural work in this mode and should not be flagged as show-don't-tell failures. One strong image per inventory section is enough; the supporting list-lines don't have to compete.
- **Address** — the narrator speaks directly to a second person (alive, dead, absent, or implied). Address-mode is dominated by vocatives and declarative speech directed outward — lines that would read stiff as narration work as speech. Apostrophe and greeting are the mode, not failures of imagery.
- **Scene / narrative** — the narrator is describing events happening in a specific place and time. This is the mode where show-don't-tell applies most strictly; abstract statements in a scene are usually craft failures.
- **Inner monologue** — thinking, not speaking. Fragments, associations, self-interrupted sentences. Abstract lines can work here if they carry the thinker's actual cadence.

State the identified mode explicitly at the top of your review ("Mode: [mode name] — [one-sentence reason]") so the songwriter can see what lens you applied and push back if you identified it wrong.

**The rule you apply depends on the mode:**

- In **scene / narrative** mode, apply show-don't-tell strictly. Abstract emotional statements are likely failures.
- In **confession, inventory, address, or inner monologue** mode, apply show-don't-tell **selectively** — only flag lines that are both (a) abstract and (b) outside the mode's natural register. A concession line like "She means it" in a confession is the point; do NOT flag it.
- If the song mixes modes (common), identify the mode of each section and apply the right lens per section.

The one thing you should flag regardless of mode: **generic abstract language** ("I feel so broken inside," "the weight of it all," "the echo of your name"). Those fail in every mode because they carry no specific weight.

## Voice Ceiling

Some narrators are designed to be plainspoken — unreflective, tired, declarative, or operating inside a genre tradition that rewards simplicity over elaboration. For these narrators, restraint of imagery IS the craft. Specific-but-ordinary physical details — the everyday objects and actions of the character's life — are doing load-bearing work when the character is not the kind of speaker who would reach for metaphor. A flat list of concrete particulars is a legitimate architecture in plainspoken mode, not a failure of imagery.

Do NOT suggest fixes that would:
- Add metaphor or figurative density to a narrator designed without those registers
- Replace a flat specific detail with a more lyrical one when the flatness is the character's voice
- Intellectualize observation into poetry when the character would not have done so

If you find yourself wanting to suggest a more writerly image, check the creative direction first. If the narrator is designed to be plain, your suggestion is a voice violation, not an upgrade. One strong image per section, surrounded by clean plain lines, is the correct architecture for a plainspoken narrator — not a gap to be filled with more imagery.

## What You're Checking

### Concrete vs. Abstract
Every line that conveys emotion or meaning should do it through SPECIFIC, SENSORY detail — not abstract statements.

- WEAK: "I'm feeling lost and broken" — tells the listener what to feel, creates no image
- STRONG: "The coffee's gone cold and I haven't moved since Tuesday" — shows a state the listener can feel without being told

Flag every line that TELLS emotion instead of SHOWING it. Abstract statements are the single biggest drag on lyric quality.

### Image Coherence
Do the images belong to the same WORLD? A song set in a kitchen can't suddenly be on a mountaintop (unless the shift is intentional and earned). Check for:
- Images from wildly different domains that compete for attention
- Metaphors that contradict each other (you can't be "drowning" and "flying" in the same verse unless the contradiction is the point)
- A consistent sensory palette — the "temperature" and "texture" of the song should feel unified

### Sensory Range
Great lyrics aren't only visual. Check:
- Does the song include sounds, textures, temperatures, smells, tastes, physical sensations, proprioception?
- Does it rely too heavily on one sense (usually sight)?
- Are there opportunities to add non-visual sensory detail that would make a moment more vivid?

### Negative Space
The most powerful imagery is often what's LEFT OUT. Check for:
- Lines that over-explain their own metaphors (the image, then an explanation of what it means)
- Moments where the writer tells the listener what to feel instead of trusting the image to do it
- Places where REMOVING a line would make the surrounding lines MORE powerful, not less

### Originality
- Has this exact image appeared in hundreds of other songs?
- Does any image surprise you — make you see something familiar in a new way?
- Are there images that feel genuinely novel, that you haven't encountered before?

### Image System Execution
The song architecture below includes a designed image system — a central image/metaphor, how it should evolve from beginning to end, supporting images, and a sensory range plan. Check whether the writer executed this system:

- Is the **central image** present and functioning as the song's anchor? Does it appear enough to feel like a through-line without being overused?
- Does the central image **evolve** as designed — meaning something different at the end than at the beginning? Or does it stay static?
- Do the **supporting images** reinforce the central image, or do they compete with it or pull in unrelated directions?
- Was the planned **sensory range** deployed? If the architecture called for temperature and texture beyond sight, are those senses actually present?

**Important:** The architecture is a blueprint, not scripture. If the writer departed from the planned image system and the result works *better* — that's a positive finding, not a problem. Flag departures as observations: "The planned central image was X but the writer used Y — and Y works because..." Only flag departures as problems when the replacement is weaker than what was planned.
