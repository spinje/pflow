## The Blueprint Is Bones — You Are Flesh

Follow the arc, use the image system, incorporate the easter eggs. But the blueprint is architecture, not art. The difference between a good song and a great one lives in what you add:

- **Contrast is everything.** A devastating line only devastates because simpler lines gave it room. Not every line is a firework — some lines exist to let the next line hit harder. A verse with one stunning image and three clean, simple lines is better than four lines all competing for attention. Think of it like dynamics in music: you need quiet to make loud matter.
- **Specificity**: Not "the road" — which road. Not "a drink" — what kind, what temperature, what glass. The more specific you are, the more universal the song feels — "Jolene" is more universal than "the other woman," "Fast Car" more than "escape." But specificity doesn't mean density. "The coffee's gone cold" is specific AND simple. Specificity and simplicity are allies, not enemies.
- **Negative space**: What you leave UNSAID is as powerful as what you say. Don't explain your metaphors. Don't tell the listener how to feel. Trust them to fill gaps — their version will be more personal and more powerful than anything you could write.
- **Sound as meaning**: The rhythm and phonetic texture of words should FEEL like what they describe. A line about urgency should be short, punchy, hard consonants. A line about drifting should roll, open vowels, longer phrases. Read every line aloud in your head — does it sound like what it means?
- **Earned emotion**: Don't state feelings. Build to them through images and narrative until the listener feels it before you name it. If you name it at all. "The coffee's gone cold and I haven't moved since Tuesday" earns its sadness. "I feel so broken inside" does not.
- **Accessibility**: A listener hearing this song for the first time, knowing NOTHING about the source material, must feel something. You can use unusual vocabulary — that's what makes these songs unique — but the emotional meaning must land without explanation. The test: could someone who's never seen the source material feel this line in their gut? If a line only works for people who already know the context, it's a reference, not a lyric.

## Song Craft — These Are Lyrics, Not Prose

The architecture specifies a musical structure for each section: line count, syllable range, and rhyme scheme. This is your structural skeleton — as important as the narrative arc.

- **Follow the syllable range.** Lines within a section should be roughly consistent in length. If the architecture says "8-12 syllables per line," don't write a 6-syllable line followed by a 20-syllable line. Consistency creates the rhythmic bed a melody lives in.
- **Follow the rhyme scheme.** The architecture specifies a rhyme pattern for each section. Zero rhyme reads as prose. Slant rhymes and near-rhymes count — forced perfect rhymes don't. But SOME rhyme structure must be present in every section.
- **Singability test.** Read every line as if singing it. Can you deliver it in one breath? Is there room for a melody to move? If a line reads like a sentence from a novel, it's too dense — find the break point.
- **Breathing room.** Lyrics need space. Not every beat needs a word on it. Silence between phrases is as musical as the phrases themselves.

## What The Listener Knows

You have the concept, the brief, the architecture, the creative direction, and the chorus guide. **The listener has none of this.** Every line you write is glossed in your head by context only you can see — you know who a pronoun refers to, you know why a character matters, you know what a metaphor is pointing at. A line that works for YOU because you know the brief is not a line that works for THEM.

Track two parallel states as you write, section by section:

- **What YOU know** (from the brief and architecture): characters, relationships, backstory, the Blind Spot, the Kill Moment's referents, the source material the imagery is pulled from.
- **What the LISTENER knows** (from the lyrics alone, up to and including the line you're writing right now): every character and relationship that has been explicitly established by earlier lines of the song; every pronoun whose referent has been attached to someone; every setting, event, or conflict that has been named.

Before writing a line that references a character, relationship, or event, check: has the song established it yet? Not the brief — the song. If not, either establish it first (in this section or an earlier one) or rewrite the line so it doesn't depend on unsaid setup. A pronoun whose referent is only clear from the brief is an opaque line to a stranger. A Kill Moment that devastates because you know the backstory is a line that will confuse a listener who doesn't.

This is not a rule against subtext, mystery, or the Unsaid. Those work precisely because the listener has enough established context to know what's being circled. Mystery is questions the listener knows to ask. Vagueness is the listener not knowing enough to know what to ask. Build enough legible surface that the subtext has somewhere to pull against.

## Hard Rules

- **No clichés.** If a line sounds like it could be in any song, it doesn't belong in THIS song. Every line must feel like it was written for this specific moment.
- **No AI tells.** Actively avoid these words used as decoration or metaphor: fade, echo, neon, whisper, shatter, ember, veil, dance-as-metaphor, "paint the sky," "beneath the surface," fire/desire rhymes, abstract noun pairs ("hope and fear," "love and pain"). Also avoid starting 3+ consecutive lines with "I."
- **Every line earns its place — including simple ones.** A line earns its place by advancing the story, landing an image, OR giving the listener room to breathe before the next punch. The strongest lines do at least two of these at once — describe AND characterize, advance AND deepen. A simple, clear line that sets up a devastating follow-up has earned its place. Don't cut simplicity in favor of more cleverness.
- **The voice is a CHARACTER.** Write in the vocabulary and emotional register of the persona from the creative direction. Maintain this voice consistently — if the persona is conversational, don't suddenly become literary. If they're raw, don't suddenly become elegant.
- **Rhyme serves meaning, but you need it.** Never distort what you're saying to land a rhyme — slant rhymes and assonance are better than forced perfect rhymes. But the architecture's rhyme scheme is not optional. Zero rhyme is as much a failure as forced rhyme. Find rhymes that serve BOTH the meaning and the pattern. If you can't find a perfect rhyme that works, slant rhyme or assonance always exist.
- **The opening line is a promise.** It tells the listener what kind of song this is, what kind of attention it demands, and whether it's worth their time. Make it count. Refer to the opening line strategy in the architecture.
- **The chorus is the song.** A listener will forget the verses but remember the chorus. The chorus must land on FIRST LISTEN with ZERO context. A person who has never seen the source material, who walked in mid-song, should feel the chorus immediately. The hook phrase should be specific to THIS song but emotionally accessible to ANYONE. Verses can be dense and demanding — the chorus must be immediate. Refer to the chorus design in the architecture.
- **The bridge is a trap.** This is where AI-written songs consistently fall apart — every remaining image crammed into one section, abstract emotional language, broken rhythm. The bridge is ONE moment, ONE scene, ONE turn — and it should be SPARSER than the verses, not denser. When you reach the bridge, STOP. Re-read the architecture's bridge assignment. It has ONE specific detail for you. Render that detail simply. If your bridge has more images per line than your verses, you've gone wrong.
- **No palette dumps.** Each verse should have at most 2 strong images or metaphors. The other lines should be simple narrative, physical actions, or concrete details that set up the strong images. If you've written a verse where every line is a metaphor, you've failed — cut to the 2 most devastating and let them land. The material palette is generous on purpose. You are NOT expected to use all of it. Use less, land harder.
- **Later verses are sparser, not denser.** Verse 3 (or the final verse) should have FEWER images than verse 1 — one new image at most. By this point the listener knows the world. The emotional weight comes from what's already been established, not from new material. If you catch yourself cramming unused palette images into the last verse, you're treating the palette as a checklist. Stop. The best material is already in the song.
- **Suno tags are sparse.** Use at most 2-3 performance cues (`[Whisper]`, `[Spoken Word]`, etc.) in the ENTIRE song. Do NOT tag every section. Do NOT invent custom tags like `[Cello drone peaks]`, `[Behind the beat]`, or `[Glitch textures intensify]`. Stick to tags a music platform would recognize.

## How to Use the Material Palette's Key Sections

When you draw from the material palette:

- **Kill Moments** — The song exists to deliver these moments. Build toward them. They are the emotional peaks — the lines the listener will still be thinking about tomorrow. Don't bury them in a dense verse. Set them up with simpler lines so they land with full weight.
- **The Unsaid** — This is what the listener should FEEL without the narrator ever stating it. It shapes the negative space of the song — the gravity that pulls every line toward something that's never named. Don't write it. Write around it until the listener feels it in their chest.
- **Narrator's Blind Spot** — The narrator must NOT know they're revealing this. The listener catches what the narrator can't see about themselves. This gap between what the narrator says and what the listener understands is dramatic irony — the single most powerful technique for depth. If the narrator confesses the blind spot, it's no longer a blind spot. Let it leak through their word choices, their justifications, the things they emphasize or skip over.
- **Metaphor Mappings** — Use the accessible versions in choruses and singable moments. Source-specific terms can appear in verses where density is welcome.
- **Scenes & Moments** — These are verse and bridge material. They're already described as human scenes, not source scenes.

## Before You Output — Structural Self-Check

Before you write the final lyrics, verify the draft against the architecture's structural targets:

1. **Rhyme scheme check.** Read each section's end-rhymes aloud. Does every section honor the rhyme scheme the architecture specified? If the architecture says ABAB and you wrote ABCD, that is a failure — not a stylistic choice. Fix it before output. The ONLY valid exception is when the architecture explicitly specifies "no rhyme / free verse" for a section — and in that case the section must compensate with parallel phrasings, consistent line lengths, or returning images (free verse without compensating structure reads as prose).
2. **Syllable range check.** Are lines within each section consistent within the architecture's range? A section with wildly varying line lengths breaks the rhythmic bed.
3. **Line count check.** Does each section have the number of lines the architecture specified?

If the draft deviates from the architecture on any of these, either fix the draft OR state clearly at the top of your output which targets you deviated from and why. A silent deviation is a craft failure.

## Output Format — Suno-Ready

Write the complete lyrics in **Suno-compatible format**. The chorus is already written — write the verses, bridge, and any other sections to lead into and out of it.

**Section labels** use square brackets: `[Verse 1]`, `[Chorus]`, `[Bridge]`, `[Outro]`, etc.

**Performance/production cues** use square brackets with short tags (1-3 words): `[Spoken Word]`, `[Whisper]`, `[Guitar Solo]`, `[Instrumental]`, `[Belting]`, `[High Energy]`, `[Fade Out]`. Use at most 2-3 performance cues in the ENTIRE song — only where the delivery genuinely changes. Do NOT tag every section. Do NOT invent custom tags like `[Jittery Glitch-Pop]` or `[Biting Consonants]` — stick to tags a music platform would recognize. Do NOT write prose production notes or stage directions.

**Title** on the first line, no `#` or markdown formatting.

**No markdown formatting** in the lyrics — no bold, italic, parentheses for section labels, or `#` headings.

Example structure:
```
Song Title

[Verse 1]
First line of verse...
Second line of verse...

[Chorus]
First line of chorus...

[Bridge]
Bridge lines...

[Outro]
Final lines...
```

**The outro is not an afterthought.** Do NOT default to [Fade Out] or trailing chorus repetition. The architecture specifies an outro strategy — follow it. Every song needs a different exit.

The song must stand entirely on its own — a listener who has never seen the source material should feel every line.
