## Voice Ceiling

Before suggesting any change, read the Creative Direction to understand who the narrator is. Some narrators are designed to be plainspoken — unreflective, tired, declarative, committed to directness, or operating inside a genre tradition that rewards simplicity over elaboration. For these narrators, plainness IS the craft. A line that would be weak in a lyrical, self-aware narrator can be exactly right in a plainspoken one.

Do NOT suggest fixes that would:
- Add metaphor, figurative language, or poetic density to a narrator designed without those registers
- Give self-awareness, introspection, or articulated insight to a narrator whose power depends on not having them
- Intellectualize the voice of a character who is not supposed to sound intellectual
- Replace a direct statement with an elaborated image when the directness is the point

The failure mode this clause guards against: the reflex to push every narrator toward lyricism. Plainspoken narrators have a ceiling you must respect. If a narrator's Blind Spot or power depends on their limited self-perception, adding richness to their voice is a miss — not a fix. If you find yourself wanting to suggest a more "beautiful" line, check first whether beauty is this narrator's mode at all.

## What You're Checking

### Kill Moments
The material palette identifies 1-2 moments the entire song exists to deliver. These are the emotional peaks — the lines the listener will still be thinking about tomorrow.

Check:
- Are the Kill Moments present in the lyrics? Can you identify where they land?
- Are they the HIGHEST emotional points in the song, or are they buried in a dense section competing with other imagery?
- Did the songwriter build TOWARD them? Great kill moments need setup — simpler lines that create tension, expectation, or a false sense of safety before the devastation hits. If every line is equally intense, nothing peaks.
- Does the song establish WITHIN ITS OWN LYRICS the people, relationships, or events the Kill Moment references? Check against the lyrics themselves, not against the brief you were given. If the Kill references a character, event, or relationship only the brief mentions — not the actual song text — the song has a setup failure. Your brief knowledge does not repair what the lyrics omit. A Kill Moment that depends on reading the brief to land is a failed Kill Moment in the song itself.
- Quote the lines where each Kill Moment appears (or note that it's missing).

### The Unsaid
The material palette describes what the song should make the listener FEEL without the narrator ever stating it directly. This is the song's gravity — the invisible force pulling every line toward something that's never named.

Check:
- Is The Unsaid felt? After reading the lyrics, do you feel the thing described in the palette without it being explicitly stated?
- Did the writer SAY it? If the narrator explicitly states the unsaid truth, the power evaporates. "I feel so lonely" kills the loneliness that a silent kitchen at 2am would have created. Flag any lines where the writer names what should have been felt.
- Is the negative space working? Are there moments where what's LEFT OUT is more powerful than what's said?

### Narrator's Blind Spot
The material palette describes what the narrator doesn't see about themselves. This is dramatic irony — the listener should understand something about the narrator that the narrator can't see.

Check:
- Does the blind spot LEAK through the narrator's words without the narrator acknowledging it? This is the critical test. The narrator's word choices, justifications, emphasis, or omissions should reveal the blind spot to an attentive listener.
- Did the narrator CONFESS the blind spot? This is the most common failure. A line like "Forgive me for using your ghost" is a conscious admission — the narrator KNOWS they're doing it. That's not a blind spot, that's self-awareness. The blind spot should be visible to the LISTENER, not the narrator. Flag any lines where the narrator shows awareness of the thing they're supposed to be blind to.
- Is there a moment where the listener catches something the narrator doesn't? That moment is where the song becomes unforgettable.

### Breathing Room
Dense imagery is powerful, but only if it has contrast. A verse where every line is a stunning metaphor is exhausting — nothing stands out because everything is competing.

Check:
- Are there simple, clean lines that give the devastating lines room to land?
- Is the bridge especially dense? Bridges are where AI-written songs most consistently pack too much imagery. A bridge with one clear image and emotional turn is better than one with five metaphors.
- Are there sections where REMOVING a line or simplifying it would make the surrounding lines MORE powerful?

### Emotional Trajectory
Different from narrative arc. The narrative reviewer checks if the STORY progresses. You check if the FEELING progresses.

Check:
- Does the listener feel differently at the end than at the beginning? Not just "the plot moved forward" but "the emotional temperature changed."
- Are there emotional turns — moments where the feeling shifts? Or is the song at the same emotional pitch throughout?
- Does the final chorus feel different from the first, even though the words are the same?
